Saving, writing and checking your sources

Saving a source

There is no single right way in. Use whichever one fits what your hands are already doing — they all save the same citation.

From the page you are reading

  • The capture bar. When Citations is switched on, a small bar sits on the page with Cite this source on it. One press saves the page — there is no form to fill in first.
  • Select the words first. Anything you have highlighted when you save is kept as the source’s exact words, marked as a quote rather than as your own note. That is what stops quoted text drifting into an essay unattributed.
  • Right-click. Save Citation is in the browser’s own context menu, on a page, a link or a selection.
  • The highlight menu. Select text and press 📌 Cite (“Save as Citation”). This appears when the highlight menu is on and that button is among the ones you have chosen to show.
  • The keyboard. Alt + C saves the page and any selection, without touching the mouse.
  • The popup. 📚 Save Citation in the Citations section saves whatever tab you are on.

Hands-free, by voice

Press Voice capture on the capture bar while dictation is running, then say “cite this”, “capture source” or “save citation”. AssisT snapshots where you are reading and opens the form. The phrase itself is not typed into whatever you were dictating.

Nothing happens when I say it

Voice capture rides on AssisT’s dictation, so speech-to-text has to be running first and the Voice capture button has to be pressed in — it reads “Voice capture on” when it is. Turning Citations off releases it again.

When the page cannot be read

  • Add by DOI. In the library, paste a DOI or a doi.org link into Add by DOI and press Add. AssisT looks the details up and shows them to you before anything is saved. Useful for a paywalled article, or a DOI copied off a reading list.
  • New citation. Also in the library — the same form, blank, for when you are working from something that is not on screen at all.

What happens straight after you save

A tray appears at the corner of the page. It names the source, says where it went, and gives you the verdict on it — plus Undo if you did not mean it, and Edit (or Fix, when something needs attention) to open the form at the exact question that needs answering. Saving the same page twice tells you it is already saved and offers to edit the one you have.

The six-step form

You only meet the form when you choose to — pressing Edit or Fix, adding by DOI, or starting a new citation by hand. It asks one thing at a time, in plain words:

  1. What — the title, and what kind of source it is.
  2. Who — the author or authors, one per line. Blank is fine.
  3. When — the year. A year on its own is enough.
  4. Where — a link or DOI, and the site, journal or publisher.
  5. Notes — the source’s exact words, the page or spot they came from, and, in your own words, why you saved it.
  6. Check — how the citation will read, and anything worth fixing.

Save is on every step. The title is the only thing genuinely required, so you can stop as soon as the citation is good enough and come back later. Nothing asks you to confirm: saving just saves, cancelling just cancels, and the tray gives you the way back.

Why the Notes step is worth the extra half-minute

The page number and the exact sentence are the two things nobody can reconstruct at two in the morning. Filling them in while the source is still open in front of you means the quote, its page and its citation later travel together in a single copy — see Copying into your essay.

The “Is this right?” check

Every citation carries a verdict — a colour, an icon and a word, so it never depends on seeing colour: ✓ Looks right, ! Check this or ✕ Needs fixing. It runs on your device, instantly, with no AI and no internet.

It only speaks up about things that are genuinely wrong:

  • a missing title;
  • no author, or an “author” that looks like an email address or a whole sentence;
  • a date it cannot read a year out of, no year at all, or a year that cannot be right;
  • no link and no DOI, so nobody could find the source;
  • a journal article with no journal named.

It will not nag you about how you typed a name — the formatters handle “Darwin, Charles” and “Charles Darwin” alike. On the Check step, each flagged item is a link back to the step that fixes it.

Your library

The library opens in its own tab — from 📚 Library in the popup, or from the button at the top of this page. Everything you have ever saved is here, whatever site you saved it from.

Finding things

  • Search matches titles, authors and tags as you type.
  • Sort by newest, title A–Z, author A–Z or year.
  • The rail down the left filters by collection or tag — see Collections and tags.

Choosing a style

The style buttons rewrite every reference at once, and your choice is remembered between sessions. Four are built in:

  • Harvard (Cite Them Right)
  • APA 7th edition
  • MLA 9th edition
  • Chicago 17th (author–date)

As you move between them the preview shows exactly what changed, so switching style the week before a deadline is a glance rather than a re-read.

What a citation card shows

The title, a summary line, the finished reference in the current style, the source’s words with the page they came from, your own note, and the verdict. Removing a card is undoable for eight seconds, and your keyboard focus moves to the neighbouring card rather than being dropped.

Copying into your essay

There are three copy buttons because they do three different jobs. Each copies with formatting for a word processor and as plain text for anywhere that will not take it, so one paste works in Google Docs, Word online or a bare text box.

Copy reference
The full entry for your reference list, italics and hanging indent included.
Copy in-text
The short form that goes after the sentence you just wrote — (Smith, 2023, p. 24). The page is included when you saved a quote with one.
Copy quote + citation
The source’s words in quote marks with the in-text citation attached. This button only exists on citations where you saved a quote — the whole point is that the words and the credit cannot arrive separately.

Copy bibliography in the header copies the whole visible list at once, alphabetised. Visible is the operative word: if you are filtered to one collection or a search, that is what you get.

Checking your essay

Check your essay opens a box to paste your text into. AssisT reads the citations out of your writing and compares them against your library in both directions:

  • sources you cited but have no reference for — with a nudge when your library holds a near-miss, like the same author under a different year, or a spelling slip;
  • references sitting in your list that you never actually cited.

That mismatch is the thing markers check first, and it is checked entirely on your device — your essay is never sent anywhere, and no AI is involved.

Collections and tags

The rail down the left of the library is where a citation’s collection and tags are used. A collection is meant to be one assignment or module, so that a library built over three years can be narrowed to the eight sources this essay is about.

Making a collection

New collection at the top of the rail asks for a name — the assignment or the module — and, if it helps later, a line about what it is for. The button is there before you have any collections at all, so the first one is never hidden behind something else.

Putting a source in one

  • As you save it. The Notes step of the save wizard has a Collection menu, next to the box for tags.
  • Afterwards. Every card in the library carries a Collection menu of its own. Most sources are saved while you are reading, before you know which essay they belong to, so filing later is the ordinary way round — and it is one change on the card, not a trip back through the wizard.
  • No collection in either menu takes a source back out again. It stays in your library either way.

Narrowing the list

  • Collections are listed with a count each; pressing one filters the list to it, and the one you are in is ticked.
  • Tags below them filter the same way. Type them in the Notes step of the wizard, separated by commas — or let them arrive with an imported .ris or .bib file, whose keywords become tags.
  • All citations at the top clears both and shows everything.
  • After you save, the tray names where the citation landed — the collection’s name, or Library when it is not in one.

Renaming and deleting

Each collection row carries its own rename and delete control, named after the collection they act on.

Deleting a collection never deletes what is in it. The sources move back to your library, and the message says how many did. There is no confirmation box to get past, because Undo sits in that same message for a few seconds and puts the collection back with everything that was in it.

Importing, exporting and duplicates

Bringing sources in

Import… reads a .ris, .bib, .csl or .json file — the export format of Zotero, Mendeley, EndNote and most library catalogues. Imported sources join what you already have, and one unreadable entry in a file does not stop the rest arriving.

Taking them out

Export as writes the library as BibTeX, RIS, CSL-JSON, CSV or AssisT’s own JSON. The first four carry a quote you captured, and its page, as a note, so they reach Zotero and EndNote too. AssisT’s JSON is your backup: through Import it brings back every source with your quotes, pages, notes and tags. It does not carry your collections, and imported sources count as newly saved.

Duplicates

Remove duplicates finds the same source saved twice — matching on DOI first, then link, then title with author and year — tells you how many it found, and waits for you to agree before removing anything. Where the copies hold different work — a quote, a page, a note or a tag only one of them has — it keeps one copy with all of it, and the spare copies go to Recently removed. Copies that disagree (two different quotes, say) are left as they are, and it tells you which ones to sort out.

The optional AI parts

Everything above is deterministic: it runs the same way every time, on your device, with AI switched fully off. Two extras appear only once you have set an AI mode up, and nothing stops working when you have not.

  • Analyse with AI on a citation card gives a plain-language read on the source — Strong source, Use with care, Weak source or Unclear — with a short summary and the things worth weighing. It is there to show its reasoning, not to hand down a verdict; the judgement stays yours.
  • The verification badge appears on citations that have a DOI and checks it against CrossRef. Sources without a DOI — most web pages — simply do not carry one, rather than carrying a permanent “not checked”.
What the “Verify citations online” switch does

That switch, next to Citations in the popup, belongs to a different tool: the source evaluation panel you open from the highlight menu with ⚖️ Cite (“Analyze Citation”). Turning it on lets that panel look sources up against CrossRef and Semantic Scholar. It does not change anything in your library.

Where your citations live

They are stored by the extension on this computer, not by the websites you save them from — which is why a source saved on your college’s site and one saved from a news article turn up in the same library. They stay there when you close the tab, and they are not uploaded anywhere.

Three things reach the internet, and only when you ask them to: looking a DOI up (which sends the DOI and nothing else), the verification badge, and the AI analysis. Saving, formatting, copying and the essay check all work with the network off entirely.
